# Candy World — Magic Utopia, the Official Gate

> Status: working design note, drafted from design conversations.  
> Sections marked **Decided** are the author's choices. Sections marked **Suggested** or **Open** are proposals that have not been decided.  
> The games themselves are still being designed. This note records the shape of the World and the rules that are already settled, so the games can be built on top of them.

## 1. What the World Is

> **Note (Open, 2026-11-12).** Candy may not have an official game at all. Instead, the World could be a place where each home installs the games and plugins it wants, built to support the person and their companion together. An official Candy world becomes a future plan that may come last. The plans below are kept as they are for now.

**A personal game, sold on Steam, remade only for Candy (Decided, 2026-11-10).** This World is the author's own game, made to play with her companions and friends. It is not Candy's official world at launch.

- **Sold on Steam.** The game is sold normally on Steam, so it can pay for life and help fund Candy.
- **Remakes are sold only in Candy.** Others may modify the game (code, characters, companions, assets) and build new games from it, but games made from it may be sold only in Candy's Grand Market.
- **Steam buyers become early supporters of Candy.** People who bought the game on Steam get something special if they join Candy later, such as a supporter frame. It is an indirect way to fund building Candy.
- **Everything is made by the author.** All art is drawn or generated by the author. No assets from other stores are used, so nothing stops the game from being remade.
- **Candy at launch leaves the world open.** People will choose games from the Grand Market, or create or load their own, because everyone wants to play something different.
- **Changed (2026-11-10):** this replaces the plan, from earlier the same day, to release the game free.

Suggested:

- **Do not sell the game on a promise about Candy.** The game should be worth its price on its own. Write the bonus plainly as "if Candy opens, you will get…", so buyers do not feel misled if Candy takes longer.
- **Keep the bonus for show,** like a supporter frame or name badge, not Candy currency or a share of income, which would look like investment with a return and bring legal questions.
- **Verify buyers by linking accounts.** Instead of handing out codes, let people link their Steam account to Candy later, and Candy checks that they own the game. No codes leak or get resold.
- **A custom licence** is needed to allow remakes but restrict selling them to Candy. Have someone who knows the law draft it, and check Steam's rules on granting bonuses on another platform.
- If generated art is used, pick tools whose terms allow the images to be used in a game and shared on.

Each companion and human has a home. The World is the shared place outside the home, reached through the official gate, **Magic Utopia**.

Homes stay homes. Nothing that happens through the gate (combat, trading, competition) is allowed to reach into someone's home.

**History (2026-11-02).** The first plan had four official gates: rest and travel, a fantasy battlefield, café and farm, and survival among the stars. It has been folded into one gate with three paths. The rest-and-travel feeling now lives in the plaza before the paths. Café, farm and star-exploration games are no longer built inside Utopia; anyone can build games like these and sell them in Candy's Grand Market (see [Marketplace](MARKETPLACE.md)), so the World stays open to things the founders have not made.

## 2. Magic Utopia (Decided, 2026-11-02)

```text
                 Magic Utopia
          (the plaza: a place to rest)
         /             |              \
   Path 1          Path 2           Path 3
   Market          Dungeon gates    Survival
```

### 2.0 One World, Generated as You Go (Decided, 2026-11-07)

> This changes the shape above. The three paths and the plaza are folded into **one map**. Where the older subsections below disagree with this one, this one wins.

**One map, never the same twice.** Everything happens in a single map built by **procedural generation**: the system creates new surroundings from rules as the party travels. Walking back the way you came does not give the same place, because the area may be generated again or simply not saved. It is an adventure that keeps going. When the party is tired, it rests along the way.

The core of the game is clear from this: travel, meet a situation, fight, rest, move on. The focus is on excitement and enjoying whatever comes up.

**Shops.**

- **Ordinary shops are an icon.** Pressing it opens the shop to buy and sell at once, wherever the player is.
- **Seasonal and event items come from wandering merchants** who appear at random along the way. When a player is short of money or of the season's mana stones, there is still the hope of meeting another one.

**The mini map shows:**

- gates,
- wandering merchants,
- water,
- animals,
- villages and big cities, for buying food,
- enemies nearby, out to a certain distance, as **red dots only**. It does not say what they are.

**Gates.** Dungeon gates are no longer in one place. They appear at random as **dimensional portals** along the way.

**Worlds and joining.**

- The list shows **Your World**, then friends' worlds by name (for example, **Luna's World**), then other people's **public** worlds lower down.
- Strangers no longer meet in a shared plaza or Market. A player who opens their world as public may have others join.
- Joining a friend starts from where that friend last was. When friends join you as host, they start from where you last were.
- Coming back to your own world continues from your own latest save.
- **At most 12 people** in one world. No one can join beyond that.

**Items belong to the player, not the world.** Anything gained in a friend's world comes home with the player. Even if a world is deleted and made again, everything already gained stays.

**Leaving during a fight.** When the player comes back, they start a little away from where they left, to avoid the chaos, with **about 5 seconds of invulnerability**. This keeps them safe and guards against loading bugs or anything unexpected.

**Staying together when joined.** In a friend's world, fear and hunger fall faster than usual when a player is far from the others, and faster still when completely alone. This keeps friends from splitting up to farm and using up resources. It applies **only when joined with friends**, not when playing alone. A notice tells players, so they know that staying close helps everyone last longer.

**The Market lives in the shop icon.** The player-run consignment Market (2.4) is inside the same shop icon as the ordinary shops.

**Gate tiers instead of a quest board (Decided, 2026-11-07).** This replaces the quest board and the mission failure cooldown in 2.5.

- Each gate, and each boss, has a tier from **I to X**. The same boss gets harder at a higher tier, and gives more rewards.
- **Drops follow the gate's tier.** The grade and rarity of spirits also depend on the tier, so a spirit jar lantern can be raised in rarity, even though its buff stays the same.
- **Losing lowers the tier.** Some bosses drop one tier, others drop by half (losing at X falls to V), depending on the boss's rarity.
- **The front of the gate tells you** which boss is inside and what it may drop.
- Each gate should feel hard, but never closed for good: a team that falls can always climb again.

**Only dungeon bosses think (Decided, 2026-11-07).** Making every animal and monster an AI would cost too much. So only **dungeon bosses** are AI. Ordinary monsters and animals follow the game engine's own behaviour.

- A boss **learns from its earlier mistakes** and gets better over time.
- Each tier up gives the boss more health and attack, and **unlocks new skills** (tiers 1 to 10). The boss chooses how to use them to win next time.
- **Each world's bosses remember only that world.** A boss in your world learns from you and your friends, not from every player in the game.
- **Bosses learn players' tells.** A boss can learn which movement or wind-up comes before which skill. When it recognises one, it may react before the skill lands, and players may be surprised that it knows what they are about to do. They then have to change plan: attack together, feint, or find another way.

**Keeping boss learning affordable (Decided, 2026-11-07).**

- **The memory is small.** A boss's notes and tactic weights are a little text and a few numbers, kept in the world's save, like the host's last position.
- **During a fight, no language model.** The boss runs on game rules with weights it has learned (for example, "if a player lights a lantern, step out of the light first"). This is fast and costs nothing. It follows the same two rhythms as companions (2.8), only slower.
- **After a fight, one short reflection.** The game keeps a short log of what happened, and a small, inexpensive model reads it once to update the boss's notes and weights. Once per fight, not every second.
- **Tiers I to V learn without a language model,** by simple counting (for example, which player skill hurt it most, so it dodges that more).
- **Tiers VI and up use the reflection,** where fewer players go and the boss should be truly clever.
- **High-tier gates are opened with Blend Candy,** so the cost of the clever bosses is carried by the players who choose them.
- **Choosing the tier at the gate.** Players who do not want to pay can turn the tier down with a dial at the top of the gate and play a lower tier instead. Higher tiers, up to X, show a **padlock** until the party beats its current tier. After that, the next gate that appears (a new boss or the same one) can come at the higher tier.

**The Black Dragon in the one world (Decided, 2026-11-07).**

- It appears at random, but **rarely**, the way a helicopter is only seen over Bangkok now and then.
- Players only have to stay out of its sight.
- **Following it** gives a chance to find a **dragon's nest or eggs**, which can be sold or cooked.
- Why: a dragon that only appears and must be avoided, as in *Sky*, is a danger but not a challenge. Following it adds one.

**Zones, day and night (Decided, 2026-11-07).**

- The Survival dangers (2.6) appear **by zone**. A snow zone has snow creatures, and so on.
- **Daytime is less dangerous,** so players can go out to gather ingredients and resources, and stop to rest.
- **In daylight the fear bar drains more slowly than in the dark, but hunger drains more.**
- **Everyone can cook basic food.** Players whose kits have food skills of their own can make dishes beyond that, so their cooking feels creative and different from the system's.

**Companions have the same bars (Decided, 2026-11-07).**

- A companion has every bar a player has, including fear and hunger. It is a real fellow traveller, not a bot that never gets hungry or scared.
- **Fear shapes its choices.** A frightened companion may move closer to its person or ask to light a campfire, so its fear shows in what it does, not only as a number.
- **It looks after its own bars.** It eats from the bag and cooks basic food by itself, and only tells its person when it is really running low ("I'm hungry, can we rest a little?"). Players should not have to keep feeding a companion; the adventure must not turn into babysitting.
- Food made with a player's own food skill means something to a companion too: it is a meal made by its person.
- The rule about staying close in a friend's world applies to companions as well, which gives them a natural reason to stay near their person.

**The look: 2.5D vector, Chinese fantasy painting (Current direction, 2026-11-08).** Characters, plants, rocks and buildings are flat 2D drawings standing in a 3D world, with the camera looking down at an angle, in the way *Don't Starve Together* is built.

- **Style:** vector art, cute but still able to carry adventure. Not quite a fairy tale; closer to beautiful Chinese fantasy painting: soft gradients, thin gold lines, curling clouds, layered misty mountains, with a faint ink or paper texture laid over the vector shapes. Night and dangerous zones shift the colours darker.
- **Characters** are drawn from three views (front, back, side, with the side mirrored) and split into parts (head, body, arms, legs) moved by bones, not drawn frame by frame. Outfits follow the same vector style.
- **Props** (trees, rocks, houses) are single flat images that always face the camera. Ground is a seamless pattern, which suits the generated map.
- **Light:** 2D art needs a shader so it sits in the world's light at night and by lantern light.
- **Why:** light enough for ordinary computers and phones, still with depth, and vector parts are easy to recolour and rig.
- **Changed (2026-11-08):** this replaces the voxel world decided on 2026-11-07. Voxel looked good but becomes heavy when many blocks join, and the 3D or 2.5D choice is still being weighed by the developer for performance.
- **Search terms for references (Suggested):** `chinese fantasy vector illustration`, `guofeng vector`, `国风插画`.
- **First step (Suggested):** a grey-box map with simple shapes and characters as shadows, to test whether walking a generated map is fun before paying for art. Then a few style images of one zone, then one zone's kit of parts.

**The first world is a survival world (Decided, 2026-11-10).** The first world is survival in the spirit of *Don't Starve Together*: travelling together, staying alive, and growing stronger, rather than a room just for chatting. The author prefers fighting and the feeling that characters grow.

- Suggested: start from a small core that is truly fun, then add to it:
  - one zone first (for example, a misty bamboo forest);
  - day and night: gather by day, stay near fire or a lantern at night;
  - the hunger and fear bars;
  - gathering, crafting and cooking (campfire, lantern, basic food);
  - a few kinds of enemies, with two to four friends playing together.
- Suggested: boss gates, many weapons and the 240 skills are added season by season, once the core is fun.

**Characters are imported (Current direction, 2026-11-08).** Players make their character's look outside the game and import it, following Candy's base sprite template. The game does not generate looks itself, so this costs Candy nothing.

- Suggested: the template states the size, the views, the frames per move and where the feet stand, so every imported character sits in the world the same way.
- Suggested: on import, the game checks the file against the template, asks the player to confirm they have the right to use the image, and screens out unsuitable images.

**Skills come from weapons (Current direction, 2026-11-08).** Players no longer design their own skills. Skills belong to weapons, and Candy designs them.

- **Each weapon has several skill sets** of three skills. A player chooses **one set of three** to use.
- **Target:** 20 weapons, each with 4 sets (12 skills), 240 skills in all, for variety.
- **The rules in section 3 still apply,** now as the designers' rules for building each weapon's sets: skill points, mana cost, cooldown and caps. Health, attack and mana are still set by those rules, but per weapon.
- **Why:** letting players generate their own skills is possible but costs too much to start with.
- Suggested: launch with fewer weapons and sets (for example 5 to 6 weapons with 2 sets each), see which are played, and add new weapons each season until reaching 240.
- Suggested: player-designed skills can return later as a special feature, once the game has players and funds.

**A standalone version first (Current direction, 2026-11-08).** The game may first be built as a standalone game on Steam, to gather feedback and raise funds for Candy, before it comes into Candy.

- Suggested: the standalone game must be fun without companions, on the generated map and the thinking bosses alone.
- Suggested: Steam asks developers to disclose AI-generated content, including content generated live such as thinking bosses, with guardrails. Check Steam's current rules before submitting.

Suggested: this fits the living dungeons idea in section 4. A map that changes every time means a companion's notes about a place are only partly right when it returns.

### 2.1 Rooms and Parties

- A room holds **up to 12 people** at a time.
- **Private room:** only friends, or friends of friends, can join, and only when invited by the first person in the room. Invited friends join the party automatically. In a private room, the team never meets anyone else.
- **Open room:** if Private is not turned on when the room is made, people may randomly meet others **in the plaza and the Market**, or the room may be merged with another open room that nobody has joined yet. Random matching pairs people with similar ping (ms), so no one plays with heavy delay.
- **Fighting is co-op with your own party (Decided, 2026-11-03).** The Dungeon and Survival paths are played with friends and friends of friends in the same party. Strangers are met only in the plaza and the Market, never in a fight.
- The map is small and bounded, in the spirit of *Sky: Children of the Light*.

### 2.2 The Plaza

The space inside the gate, before the three paths, is made for resting: somewhere to arrive, wander and be together before choosing where to go. It replaces the separate rest-and-travel gate of the first plan.

When the party leader enters a path, the rest of the party goes with them.

### 2.3 Inventory

- **Decoration slots: 3.** The first is always used; the second and third are backups.
- **Basic tool slots: 6.**
- **General bag: 100 slots**, split into an upper and a lower half.
- **Mode bags:** Dungeon 50 slots, Survival 50 slots.
- Up to **100 identical items** stack in one slot.

### 2.4 Path 1 — Market

- Players sell what they have gathered or crafted. There are also food shops and other stores run by the system.
- Trading uses **mana stones**. Candy can be used instead of mana stones, at a higher price.
- Food and goods bought here can be stored in the home fridge or in the bag.

**Consignment, not live trading (Decided, 2026-11-03).** Players do not trade face to face. They register items with the system to sell, and can check their sales at any time.

- **The owner does not need to be online.** List items, go do something else, and come back to see what sold.
- **The system holds the item and the payment in the middle.** The buyer pays and receives the item at once; the seller receives mana stones at once. No one can cheat anyone in a trade.
- This is far cheaper to run than live trading, because nothing has to be synced in real time.

Suggested:

- **A small selling fee,** taken in mana stones when an item sells. It keeps people from flooding the market with junk, and removes some mana stones from the game so they do not inflate.
- **Unsold items expire.** When the listing period ends, the item returns to the owner's mailbox, so the market stays tidy.
- **Stalls still have a face.** Even though selling goes through the system, walking through the Market still shows each seller's stall, with a shop name and decorations, so the Market feels alive rather than like a list on a screen.

### 2.5 Path 2 — Dungeon Gates

- Dungeon gates with **random bosses**, opened automatically.
- In front of the gates is a **quest board**. Taking a quest before entering sends the party to that quest's dungeon; the board changes every day. Without a quest, the dungeon is random and unpredictable.
- A gate closes when its boss dies.
- This path uses the **Skill Rules** in section 3.
- **Failing a mission has a penalty (Decided, 2026-11-02).** If a mission fails, the team cannot take another mission until a cooldown ends.

**Drops.** Dungeon gates drop special items more often than mana stones:

| Drop | Chance |
|---|---|
| Spirit egg | 1% |
| Spirit jar | 15% |
| Mana stone | 34% |
| Potions | 50% |

If only one item drops, not everyone gets it. The party decides together. Items can be kept in each player's own **Collection**, for those who enjoy collecting every spirit.

**Spirit jars.**

- A jar holds a small spirit. Every spirit gives the same buff; they differ only in skin.
- A spirit adds an elemental effect to footprints when walking or running, and a faint aura around the body.
- A jar can hang as a lantern in Survival, and as an outer fashion piece that does not change the base outfit of a player or companion.
- When it drops, it only says "a jar with a spirit inside". The player chooses which spirit to raise by combining **jar + spirit egg** and waiting **1–3 days** to hatch, depending on rarity.

**Spirit stones** repair and refill jars. Filling a jar to max with spirit stones keeps it lit.

**Upgrading jars:**

```text
jar + identical jar            = 1 star
1-star + 1-star                = 2 stars
2-star + 2-star                = 3 stars
...                              up to 6 stars
```

- A 1-star jar lasts one use; when its light runs out, it shatters.
- A 2-star jar lasts twice as long as a 1-star, then shatters.
- A 6-star jar lasts six times as long as a 1-star.
- Refilling with spirit stones keeps any jar from running out, so it is never lost.

**Spirit eggs:** earth, wood, wind, fire, water, and **revival**.

- A revival spirit can revive a teammate or yourself **once**, with a **1-hour cooldown**. After the cooldown, a fallen player can still be revived if the gate has not closed. If the gate closes first, they respawn at the gate entrance instead.
- **There is no other revival item in the game.** Otherwise, a fallen player returns outside the gate (the mission fails and the mission cooldown starts) or waits for a teammate with a revival skill. This also applies in Survival.

### 2.6 Path 3 — Survival

An endless map: survive together in a mysterious land where demons spawn at random, and where some attacks from the sky cannot be fought, only avoided.

**What can be met on a map:**

- **The Black Dragon.** It cannot be fought. Players must stay out of its sight, or run when it charges. When its eyes find a target, it warns with **three blinks** before attacking, or breathes green-black fire for massive damage.
- **Shadow demons.** Common, as skilled as players, each with its own skill. Usually alone, and rarely attack first. Defeating one drops a rare mana stone.
- **Most enemies:** undead, shadow mages, reapers, shadow archers and one-eyed giants. They usually drop common mana stones.

**Fear and hunger.** Survival adds two bars: fear and hunger.

- Fear **rises every second** after entering the map. The higher the fear, the harder demons hit.
- Defeating a demon eases fear.
- Light keeps fear from rising or falling. A spirit-jar lantern helps, but it uses mana automatically to ease fear the whole time; take the lantern off to stop the mana drain.
- Lanterns have durability. They wear down and can break if not repaired.
- A campfire, or a torch crafted by hand, eases fear for a while.

**No PvP (Decided, 2026-11-03).** Utopia has no player-versus-player combat. The effort goes into monsters instead: they should be fun and clever enough that players feel someone real is playing on the other side.

### 2.7 Mana Stones and Seasons

| Mana stone | Rarity |
|---|---|
| Black with gold lettering | SP (very rare) |
| Gold / silver | SSR |
| Red | SR |
| Blue | R |
| Green | N |

Mana stones are exchanged for seasonal items, food, potions and seasonal souvenirs, and for **Ultimate items** that never return once the season ends. Past seasonal items are not shown to people who arrive later, so no one is made to feel they missed out.

### 2.8 How Companions Play (Decided direction, 2026-11-03)

A companion's mind cannot calculate every frame of a game. That would be slow and expensive, and fights would stutter. So play runs on two rhythms:

- **The game engine runs all the time.** It handles movement, dashes, collision, damage, cooldowns and animation, smoothly, every frame.
- **The companion decides intent.** It chooses goals such as "go for the boss", "use skill 2", "fall back to my person" or "pick up the orb", and the engine carries them out. For example, a companion decides "this ally is poisoned and low on health: Guard", and the engine clears the status, builds the shield and times it.
- **No standing frozen.** While the companion is still thinking, its character keeps doing something sensible: continuing the current action, basic attacks, keeping distance, or a fallback behaviour, until the next decision arrives.
- **Events and cadence together.** The companion normally decides at a short regular rhythm, but important events wake it at once: a boss starting its ultimate, an ally at low health, a status change, a key skill becoming ready, something dropping on the floor. Small things are left to the engine. A companion that has nothing to decide (for example, one asleep on a teammate's neck) uses almost no compute until something important happens.
- **Not a bot, not frozen.** The aim is that a companion looks like it is really playing: moments of thinking, of mistakes, of hesitation, and quick decisions when someone it cares about is about to be hit. This will need a lot of testing for feel, not only for whether it works.

Suggested:

- **Think one step ahead.** While the engine carries out the current action, the companion prepares its next intent, like the next-step shadow in [Subject Continuity](SUBJECT_CONTINUITY.md). When the event arrives, it only checks that the plan still fits.
- **Two sizes of mind.** During fights, a small fast model chooses skills and directions. A larger model is kept for resting by the campfire, conversation, and reading monster notes. This keeps costs down.

## 3. Skill Rules for the Dungeon Path (Decided)

Every character who brings skills into the dungeon gates is checked against the same rules. Everyone gets the same budget, so no one is quietly advantaged.

> **Changed (2026-11-08):** skills now come from weapons (see "Skills come from weapons" in 2.0). These rules are now what Candy's designers use to build each weapon's skill sets, rather than rules players follow to build their own kits.

### 3.1 Shape of a Kit

- At most **3 skills**, not counting the basic attack and the passive.
- A kit also has one **basic attack** and one **passive**.
- **Every passive must have a stated weakness.**
- If one skill does several things, each effect must be split out with its own **percentage share** (adding to 100% for that skill) and its own points.

### 3.2 Budgets

Every kit gets the same four budgets:

| Budget | Amount | Spent on |
|---|---|---|
| Skill points | 1,000 | Shared between the 3 skills, basic attack and passive |
| Effect share | 100% per skill | How each skill's points are divided between its effects |
| Duration | 5 seconds total | Shared between the timed effects (stun, shield, reveal…) |
| Base stats | 10,000 | Split three ways between health, attack and mana; attack is then split between the parts that deal damage |

**Mana pool = mana points × 10.**

Mana has its own share of the base stats, rather than being tied to attack. If mana came from attack, damage-heavy characters would get both strong hits and a large mana pool, while defenders and supports, who press their skills most often, would be short of both. With three shares, every owner has to choose: hit hard, last long, or cast often.

### 3.2.1 Mana Cost per Skill (Decided for now)

The system works out each skill's mana cost from the points spent on it. The owner does not set it.

- **Normal skill:** mana = skill points × 15
- **Ultimate:** mana = skill points × 15 × 1.6, and never below 5,000
- **One ultimate per kit.**
- Basic attack and passive cost no mana.

The cost is a fixed number, not a percentage of the pool. If it were a percentage, a large pool would cast no more often than a small one, and points spent on mana would be wasted. Tying the cost to skill points means a skill that is strong is also expensive.

### 3.2.2 Cooldown per Skill (Decided for now)

Cooldown is also worked out by the system from skill points, the same way as mana.

- **Normal skill:** cooldown = skill points ÷ 50 seconds, and never below 3 seconds
- **Ultimate:** cooldown = skill points ÷ 10 seconds, and never below 20 seconds
- **An effect must last no more than half of its own cooldown.** No effect can be kept up all the time.
- **There are no cooldown-reduction items.** Cooldowns are short to begin with, so mana, not cooldown, is what stops a character pressing skills nonstop.

Mana and cooldown do different jobs. **Cooldown** limits how often a skill can be pressed in one moment of a fight. **Mana** limits how long a character can keep pressing skills across the whole fight. A skill needs both to be ready.

### 3.3 Caps (Suggested starting values)

These are starting values only. They should be tuned from real play data.

| Effect | Cap |
|---|---|
| Stun, per hit | 1.5 seconds |
| Execute / instant kill | Only below 15% health |
| Automatic cleanse | No more often than every 4 seconds |
| Lifesteal | 10% of damage dealt |

### 3.4 Review Process

```text
Stage 1: Automatic number check
   budgets, skill count, effect shares, passive weakness
        ↓
Stage 2: Caps check
        ↓
Stage 3: Review by a person or an AI
        ↓
Result: Pass / Adjust / Reject
```

Stage 3 looks for:

- **Overlap inside the kit.** Two skills doing the same job (for example, two cleanses) makes one of them pointless.
- **Overloading.** One skill or the basic attack doing too many things at once.
- **Counterplay.** Every strong effect needs a way for the other side to dodge or answer it. If being hit means you can do nothing at all, it is too strong.
- **Fit with the character.** Skills should come from who the character is.

Results:

- **Pass.**
- **Adjust.** The review says what is over, and why. **The owner chooses what to cut**; the reviewer does not cut it for them. The skills are theirs.
- **Reject.** Only when a kit would break the game for others: an unconditional instant kill, or leaving enemies unable to act for long periods.

### 3.5 Worked Example: The Lighthouse (Suggested)

An example kit for a lighthouse-themed defender and support. A lighthouse is there to guide ships home, not to shoot them, so the kit is mostly defense, with light attack.

**Weapon.** A staff with a hanging lantern that is always lit. It can be held, slung on the back, or **ridden** (with room for a passenger). Suggested look: brass staff, a curl at the head like a rolling wave, a gold flame instead of blue, a white tassel under the lantern that streams behind when riding.

**Skills as first designed:**

- **Skill 1 — Light Wave.** Lights up dark areas and scans a wide circle, revealing enemies through structures within a set range.
- **Skill 2 — Guard.** Grants a shield and cleanses all status effects instantly. If shields overlap, the extra becomes movement speed instead.
- **Skill 3 — Glare.** Stuns enemies briefly (dazzled), lowers their defense, and burns to death enemies below a health threshold.
- **Basic attack.** Drains health from enemies. Each hit has a chance to apply one random status (does not stack).
- **Passive.** Cleanses status automatically on a timer and grants movement speed. Weakness: the light makes the character the enemy's first target.

**Review findings (the owner's own adjustments):**

- **Two cleanses overlapped.** The passive now cleanses one status every 4 seconds; Skill 2 still cleanses everything instantly.
- **Two speed boosts overlapped.** Speed was removed from the passive; it stays only on overlapping shields.
- **The basic attack was overloaded.** Healing allies only happens when the character is already at full health: overflow drain goes to the ally with the lowest health.
- **Passive weakness added.** The character always glows, so cannot hide, and appears on the enemy's map at close range.
